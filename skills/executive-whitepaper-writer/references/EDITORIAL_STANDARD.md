# Editorial Standard — Executive Whitepaper Writer

## Editorial target

Clear, connected, evidence-based prose for executives. The tone is measured; the analytical burden is high. Professionalism does not require abstract vocabulary.

## Evidence discipline

Keep a private evidence ledger during drafting:

| Evidence class | Example of safe wording | Unsafe upgrade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DOCUMENTED FACT | "The company announced a creator program." | "The creator program is failing." |
| USER-REPORTED | "The candidate reports three weeks without an update." | "The company has a systemic recruiting failure." |
| REASONED INFERENCE | "These separate initiatives could support a broader product portfolio." | "The company is building a fully integrated digital business." |
| HYPOTHESIS | "Growth could outpace settlement or support capacity." | "Growth is outpacing the company's infrastructure." |
| RECOMMENDATION | "A limited audit should establish whether the risk is material." | "The executive team must restructure immediately." |

Maintain metric classes such as delivered, estimated, forecast, projection, prototype and target without blending them. A senior-sounding sentence does not make a claim verifiable.

When cited research is provided, preserve exact traceability. Do not use made-up citations, imply a direct source supports a broader claim, or attribute the user's own interpretation to an institution. When sources are unavailable, identify the gap rather than manufacturing precision.

## Voice and word choice

Bad:
"Raheel's visionary disruption is unlocking a revolutionary second innovation flywheel across the creator ecosystem."

Better:
"The company's digital initiatives may support new products and services beyond its current creator programs."

Bad:
"I think SharkNinja has a very big architecture problem."

Better:
"Whether the existing systems can support further expansion remains to be established."

Bad:
"The organization must urgently synchronize its multi-dimensional decision intelligence fabric."

Better:
"The next step is to determine which systems already share reliable information and where decisions depend on missing evidence."

Bad:
"These initiatives create a transformational, once-in-a-generation growth opportunity."

Better:
"These initiatives create an opportunity to test additional digital products."

### Do not let objectivity become false certainty

"I think the operating model is broken" is obviously an opinion. "The operating model is broken" is an unsupported assertion if there is no evidence. A better sentence preserves the actual evidence boundary: "The proposed audit should determine whether the operating model creates material delays or gaps."

### Remove jargon in context, not through a blind word ban

Use a technical term when it refers to something precise, such as causal incrementality, event sourcing or contractual entitlement. Explain it once where needed. Avoid clusters of vague terms such as "AI-native enterprise decision orchestration transformation layer."

### Do not overhedge

One precise qualifier in the relevant sentence is better than repeating "perhaps", "potentially" and "may" through every paragraph. Facts should be written plainly. Unverified diagnoses should be identified as such.

## Full-document cohesion test

Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph consecutively. They should describe a chain of reasoning without relying on headings. Then remove a paragraph and see if the next one still follows. If it does and nothing meaningful is lost, the paragraph may be redundant.

Check the conclusion against the thesis:
- If it introduces a new business problem, establish that problem earlier.
- If it is a slogan, replace it with the actual decision or uncertainty.
- If it recommends restructuring, show which verified problem structure is meant to solve.
- If the paper is a research note, state what is still hypothetical.

## Prose-format defaults

For an executive paper, default to substantial explanatory paragraphs grouped under a small number of meaningful headings. Bullet lists may help with concrete questions, phases or recommendations; they must not carry all the analysis. Tables belong where comparison truly reduces cognitive effort. Do not substitute dashboard-style cards for sustained writing.

## Evaluation questions

A reviewer should be able to answer:
1. What does the paper claim?
2. What evidence supports the claim?
3. What is the strongest reason the claim may be wrong?
4. Why does this paragraph follow the preceding one?
5. What decision, if any, follows?
6. Which facts could be cited or checked?
7. Does any important statement sound more certain than its source?
8. Would a real executive use these words in a serious discussion?

Assess each question as supported, needs revision, or unverifiable. Do not invent a psychometrically validated score or claim that one self-edited example proves better performance.


## Author-led white paper editing: additional regression checks

An author's draft is not a defective template to be replaced by a standardized framework. Capture the author's intended reader, argument order, explicit proposal, and named priorities before revising. Preserve the scope of the claim while correcting factual overreach. If the author says a technology strategy should span commercial, physical-product and financial decisions, a rewrite that narrows the thesis to media analytics is a substantive failure, not a style improvement.

### Distinguish stylistic failure modes

| Failure | Why it fails | Revision principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Every team is winning. But is the enterprise?" | Detachable punchline rather than an operating argument | Explain the distinct mandate of each team and where its outputs must reconcile |
| "The future is a connected intelligence fabric." | Unspecified system, owner and value mechanism | Name the decision, evidence, authority, operating interfaces, economics and feedback |
| Four consecutive one-sentence paragraphs | Feels like a social article or keynote | Combine into a developed causal paragraph |
| "The company has no digital feedback loop" | Treats uninspected company systems as absent | State the required operating capability and the evidence needed to assess existing implementations |
| "AI can't close the gap" | Unqualified claim about a complex capability | Explain which constraints AI can accelerate and which contractual, semantic or ownership constraints it cannot independently remove |
| Replacing three user-defined priorities with generic consulting pillars | Discards the author's organizing thought | Preserve those priorities, then edit the dependencies among them |

### Executive perspective pattern when the author specifies it

A research white paper may open with a direct strategic proposal rather than a literature review or anecdote. Develop the case through (a) definition of the strategic discipline, (b) concrete business mechanics, (c) accelerating requirements and structural dependencies, (d) the proposed causal risk, (e) acknowledgment of existing capabilities, and (f) integrated priorities. This is an example **only**: the author's supplied ordering takes precedence. Headings such as "Executive Perspective" and "Priority 1" are legitimate if they organize an actual argument rather than replace it.

A credible recommendation can remain bold: "Treat decision architecture as a continuing strategic responsibility." A statement of empirical deficiency requires different evidence: "The company lacks end-to-end decision architecture" must not be written as established without inspection. Do not let a factual qualification weaken a strategic proposal unnecessarily.

### Final author-voice acceptance test

Compare the revised paper to the author's latest supplied example, not merely to the writer's first draft. Confirm: same thesis, same scope, same strategic priorities, same concrete mechanisms, no generic filler substituted for technical insight, no invented metrics, and no staccato thought-leadership cadence. If the writing is more polished but changes what the author is arguing, it fails.


## Active voice as the editorial default

Give each consequential claim an identifiable actor and action. Prefer "Advertising partners report media exposures, retailers record transactions, and finance reconciles contractual obligations and economic outcomes" over passive constructions that obscure responsibility.

| Less effective | More effective |
| --- | --- |
| "Financial treatment is established through reconciliation." | "Finance reconciles available evidence against the company's financial records and contractual obligations." |
| "A common operating logic is defined by decision architecture." | "Decision architecture defines a common operating logic across business functions and external partners." |
| "The next decision is informed by feedback." | "Measured outcomes inform the next decision." |
| "The establishment of enterprise alignment enables growth." | "Commercial, technology and finance leaders align requirements so existing investments support growth." |

The examples demonstrate **grammar and specificity**, not independently verified facts about a named company. Preserve the status of any case-specific assertion.

Use passive voice when its subject matters more than the actor or the actor is unknown; never invent ownership to meet a stylistic preference. Avoid nominalizations that bury action in nouns. When checking sentences, ask: **Who acts? What do they change? What evidence authorizes the action? What business outcome follows?**

### Separate Purpose from Executive Summary

A Purpose section can define the proposed discipline succinctly using the author's direct construction, such as "This paper proposes an end-to-end decision architecture." An Executive Summary should then establish the concrete operating challenge, supporting facts and limitations, architectural implications, and strategic priorities. Duplicating the Purpose is a structural defect, not stronger emphasis.
