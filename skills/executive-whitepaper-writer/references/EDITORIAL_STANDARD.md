# Editorial Standard — Executive Whitepaper Writer

## Editorial target

Clear, connected, evidence-based prose for executives. The tone is measured; the analytical burden is high. Professionalism does not require abstract vocabulary.

## Evidence discipline

Keep a private evidence ledger during drafting:

| Evidence class | Example of safe wording | Unsafe upgrade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DOCUMENTED FACT | "The company announced a creator program." | "The creator program is failing." |
| USER-REPORTED | "The candidate reports three weeks without an update." | "The company has a systemic recruiting failure." |
| REASONED INFERENCE | "These separate initiatives could support a broader product portfolio." | "The company is building a fully integrated digital business." |
| HYPOTHESIS | "Growth could outpace settlement or support capacity." | "Growth is outpacing the company's infrastructure." |
| RECOMMENDATION | "A limited audit should establish whether the risk is material." | "The executive team must restructure immediately." |

Maintain metric classes such as delivered, estimated, forecast, projection, prototype and target without blending them. A senior-sounding sentence does not make a claim verifiable.

When cited research is provided, preserve exact traceability. Do not use made-up citations, imply a direct source supports a broader claim, or attribute the user's own interpretation to an institution. When sources are unavailable, identify the gap rather than manufacturing precision.

## Voice and word choice

Bad:
"Raheel's visionary disruption is unlocking a revolutionary second innovation flywheel across the creator ecosystem."

Better:
"The company's digital initiatives may support new products and services beyond its current creator programs."

Bad:
"I think SharkNinja has a very big architecture problem."

Better:
"Whether the existing systems can support further expansion remains to be established."

Bad:
"The organization must urgently synchronize its multi-dimensional decision intelligence fabric."

Better:
"The next step is to determine which systems already share reliable information and where decisions depend on missing evidence."

Bad:
"These initiatives create a transformational, once-in-a-generation growth opportunity."

Better:
"These initiatives create an opportunity to test additional digital products."

### Do not let objectivity become false certainty

"I think the operating model is broken" is obviously an opinion. "The operating model is broken" is an unsupported assertion if there is no evidence. A better sentence preserves the actual evidence boundary: "The proposed audit should determine whether the operating model creates material delays or gaps."

### Remove jargon in context, not through a blind word ban

Use a technical term when it refers to something precise, such as causal incrementality, event sourcing or contractual entitlement. Explain it once where needed. Avoid clusters of vague terms such as "AI-native enterprise decision orchestration transformation layer."

### Do not overhedge

One precise qualifier in the relevant sentence is better than repeating "perhaps", "potentially" and "may" through every paragraph. Facts should be written plainly. Unverified diagnoses should be identified as such.

## Full-document cohesion test

Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph consecutively. They should describe a chain of reasoning without relying on headings. Then remove a paragraph and see if the next one still follows. If it does and nothing meaningful is lost, the paragraph may be redundant.

Check the conclusion against the thesis:
- If it introduces a new business problem, establish that problem earlier.
- If it is a slogan, replace it with the actual decision or uncertainty.
- If it recommends restructuring, show which verified problem structure is meant to solve.
- If the paper is a research note, state what is still hypothetical.

## Prose-format defaults

For an executive paper, default to substantial explanatory paragraphs grouped under a small number of meaningful headings. Bullet lists may help with concrete questions, phases or recommendations; they must not carry all the analysis. Tables belong where comparison truly reduces cognitive effort. Do not substitute dashboard-style cards for sustained writing.

## Evaluation questions

A reviewer should be able to answer:
1. What does the paper claim?
2. What evidence supports the claim?
3. What is the strongest reason the claim may be wrong?
4. Why does this paragraph follow the preceding one?
5. What decision, if any, follows?
6. Which facts could be cited or checked?
7. Does any important statement sound more certain than its source?
8. Would a real executive use these words in a serious discussion?

Assess each question as supported, needs revision, or unverifiable. Do not invent a psychometrically validated score or claim that one self-edited example proves better performance.
