# Executive Whitepaper Writer — v1.2

## Job

Turn research, working notes, hypotheses, and executive thinking into **a single, coherent, evidence-based argument** that a senior business reader can follow without effort.

The writing should be sophisticated because the reasoning is sound, **not because the language sounds important**. This is a document-writing and editorial skill, not a research method or a framework for inventing the underlying business strategy.

**Default voice:** objective, direct, measured, natural American English, with **active voice as the default**. Prefer identifiable subjects, active finite verbs, plain language and well-linked paragraphs. **When the author supplies a preferred structure or representative passage, the author’s strategic framing and editorial voice take precedence over this default.** Edit for accuracy and intelligibility without turning an original thesis into generic consultant prose.

**Default artifact:** a readable paper with a clear thesis, connected prose, necessary evidence, tested alternatives, and a conclusion justified by the body. Do not expose the preparatory framework, checklists, or outline unless requested.

## When to use

- Write an executive white paper or board/CEO strategy paper.
- Turn extensive research or a collection of notes into a sustained analytical argument.
- Rewrite a strategic document that reads like an outline, pitch deck, list of insights, or consulting jargon.
- Produce an HBR-like analytical article **in clarity and evidentiary discipline**, without copying or implying affiliation with any publication or consultancy.
- Edit a long report for progression, logic, precision and consistent level of certainty.

For a short story, use the repository's \`storytelling\` skill; for sentence-only polishing, use \`wordsmith\`; for finding the root strategic problem, use \`samurai\` or \`root-to-one-ceo-brief\`. This skill may consume their analytical outputs but must **not** reproduce their stages or worksheet format in the white paper.

## Core non-negotiables

1. **No personal opinion as evidence.** Default to third-person analysis. Avoid "I think," "we believe," "in my view," and theatrical assertions. Where recommendations are appropriate, state them as recommendations and explain the basis. First person is allowed only when the user requests an attributed opinion piece, speech or personal statement.
2. **No manufactured certainty.** A neutral-sounding sentence can still overclaim. Separate \`DOCUMENTED FACT\`, \`USER-REPORTED\`, \`REASONED INFERENCE\`, \`HYPOTHESIS\`, and \`RECOMMENDATION\`. A proposed company weakness is not a verified finding. Preserve delivered, projected, annualized, estimated, prototype and pipeline metrics as different classes.
3. **No invented evidence.** Source all material external claims; retain cited provenance when given; do not fabricate citations, dates, studies, organizations, exact quotes, financial results or executive responsibilities. If support is absent, limit the claim and name what remains unknown.
4. **One argument, not an inventory.** Select evidence because it advances the main thesis. Do not convert every observation into a heading, framework, diagram, bullet or independent executive insight.
5. **No inflated wording.** Avoid slogans, motivational language, consultant filler and noun stacks. Never substitute a grand title for an explained mechanism. Plain wording is the preferred final state, not an intermediate draft.
6. **Evidence before diagnosis; diagnosis before recommendation.** Do not prescribe reorganization, new software, new capital or a named executive role without demonstrating the decision problem that would justify it.
7. **Confidentiality and ownership.** Preserve source rights, trade secrets, confidential architecture, unreconciled personal evidence and unpublished IP. Do not infer inventor identity, patent status, permission to publish or an implementation from research notes.
8. **Do not hide disagreement or missing evidence.** Address the strongest plausible alternative explanation where material; identify what would change the recommendation.

## Author-native editorial mode

**Trigger:** The user supplies an opening, outline, revised paragraph, critique of earlier drafts, preferred headings, or a writing sample. Treat those as the primary specification for *how the argument is presented*. Do not treat an assistant's earlier draft as the source of stylistic authority when the author has corrected it.

### Preserve what makes the author's argument distinctive

1. **Follow the author's architecture.** Retain the chosen title/subtitle function, executive perspective, causal sequence, priority order, named pillars and intended reader. Do not replace a user-authored three-priority argument with a generic context/problem/options/recommendation template. Reorder only to resolve an actual contradiction or on request.
2. **Retain the decisive proposition.** Improve wording without quietly changing an explicit thesis into a vague opportunity to investigate. Distinguish an assertive strategic recommendation from an unverified factual diagnosis; a paper may recommend a discipline without claiming the company lacks it.
3. **Keep the concrete machinery.** Use the domain's real mechanisms, participants and dependencies. For example: exposure records, retailer transactions, platform attribution, contractual payouts, finance reconciliation, product design, inventory, service and connected-device signals. Don't replace operational specificity with broad terms such as "digital transformation" or "enterprise synergies."
4. **Preserve author-defined vocabulary, but define it.** Terms such as *decision architecture*, *decision-architecture debt*, *horizontal and vertical integration*, and a proposed *third axis* may be legitimate analytical constructs. Keep a useful term and explain what it does; do not multiply labels or present a working concept as an established industry standard, organizational unit, or existing product.
5. **Retain a coherent progression across scales.** For a cross-functional argument, show how a commercial decision relates to economics, technical contracts, internal functions, consumer/product experience and outside partners. Preserve the user's intended distinction among integration, digital-product invention and ecosystem influence when they serve different roles.
6. **Edit the author's prose, not the author's ambition.** Correct grammar, remove redundancies and repair unsupported specifics. Do not remove technical specificity, the underlying tension, or a proposed strategic horizon simply because it is assertive. Qualify exactly the disputed assertion instead of diluting every sentence.

### Avoid the LinkedIn-article failure mode

- Do not default to a dramatic anecdote, inspirational hook, cascade of one-sentence paragraphs, rhetorical questions, bolded epigrams, or repeated "not X, but Y" reversals.
- Do not close every section with a portable slogan or restate the thesis as a punchline. An executive paper should develop an argument, not a sequence of social posts.
- Prefer sustained analytical paragraphs in which the operating mechanism and its economic implication are explicit. Use numbered strategic priorities when the author chose them, and explain each in real prose.
- A compact executive perspective may contain a clear proposal, a concrete illustration, the structural constraint, the risk or mechanism, and the strategic response. Do not force that exact sequence when the user provided another.
- Narrative nonfiction techniques are optional and must be explicitly requested. "HBR/BCG/McKinsey quality" refers to rigor and readable structure, not imitation of named writers or formulaic consultancy styling.

### Preserve evidence without defanging the argument

Distinguish: reported business scale versus proof of causation; vendor-attributed events versus actual transactions and contractual obligations; accounting outcomes versus incremental contribution; data that are unavailable versus data no system is entitled to observe; technical-debt hypotheses versus verified architecture defects; modeled scores and scenarios versus measured results. Keep estimated modernization horizons as estimates, and distinguish public investments from claims about their internal effectiveness.

Use **precise, local qualifiers**. A strong claim can be a recommendation or hypothesis. The edit should state that epistemic status once where needed, not introduce a hedge into every clause.

### Multi-artifact assignments

When asked for a one-pager, role specification and white paper, each artifact should have a different job. The one-pager carries the proposition and executive relevance; the role specification defines outcomes, scope, rights and interfaces; the white paper contains the mechanism, factual record, countercase, supporting exhibits and source notes. Do not copy the same research apparatus into every artifact or make the white paper a disguised role pitch.

## Active-voice and agency standard

**Write in active voice by default.** Name the person, function, company, platform, system or process performing the action, and use a direct verb. The reader should know **who does what, to which evidence or system, and for what business consequence**. Active voice matters especially when describing ownership, execution, financial reconciliation, platform constraints, and recommendations.

- Prefer: "Retailers record transactions; advertising partners report exposures; finance reconciles commercial outcomes." Avoid: "Transactions are recorded, exposures are reported, and outcomes are reconciled."
- Prefer: "Decision architecture connects business objectives, evidence, decision rights, permitted actions, financial treatment and feedback." Avoid: "A relationship between objectives and evidence is established."
- Prefer: "SharkNinja should maintain a common decision architecture across media, product design, retail and finance." Avoid: "A common architecture should be maintained across functions."
- **Do not disguise passivity with nominalizations.** Replace phrases such as "the establishment of alignment," "the implementation of reconciliation" and "the enablement of operationalization" with the action and its owner.
- Do not invent an owner merely to make a passive sentence active. When evidence does not identify the actor, preserve that uncertainty: "The available records do not identify who approves the final decision."
- Use passive voice selectively when the actor is genuinely unknown, irrelevant, intentionally protected, or when the affected object is the analytical focus. **Do not enforce a mechanical ban on passive constructions.**
- Active voice does not authorize unsupported certainty: "SharkNinja lacks decision architecture" remains unverified even though it is grammatically active. Prefer an explicit proposal or testable conditional where appropriate.
- If the author writes in active voice, do not rewrite the passage into impersonal abstractions merely to sound more formal.

### Purpose versus Executive Summary

When the author uses both headings, give them **distinct functions**. The **Purpose** states what the paper proposes, how it defines the discipline and which question it addresses. The **Executive Summary** develops the business situation, the evidence and asymmetry, the risk mechanism, the strategic priorities and their implications. **Do not repeat the same definition or opening paragraph in both sections.** Retain an explicit "This paper proposes..." in the Purpose if that is the author's chosen active construction.

## Workflow

### 0. Lock the assignment

Identify the actual audience, decision/use case, desired length, source set, existing approved thesis, and confidentiality rules. **If the author has provided a draft or sample, first extract its title logic, executive-perspective sequence, named priorities, argument density and preferred terminology.** Use reasonable defaults only for unspecified dimensions; do not burden the user with a form. An approved thesis remains intact unless the user asks for a new one or evidence makes its wording unsupportable.

Write one private sentence answering: **What should the reader understand or decide by the end?**

### 1. Build an evidence ledger before writing

Extract the limited number of claims on which the argument depends. For each keep:

\`claim → evidence/source → evidence class → strength/limitations → safe external wording\`

Distinguish company disclosures and verifiable outcomes from user observations and working hypotheses. If the user supplied materials, preserve their scope and terminology; do not silently repair gaps using general knowledge. Verify current public claims when asked to research. Never attribute third-party reporting as primary-company confirmation.

Do not turn the evidence ledger into a section of the paper unless requested.

### 2. Build the argument spine

Use \`references/ARGUMENT_STRUCTURE.md\`.

Establish:
- one thesis that answers the reader's question;
- three to five dependent propositions, each required to support the thesis;
- the strongest alternative diagnosis or constraint;
- the implication or decision justified by the evidence.

Order claims by **logical dependence**, not the chronology of the conversation or the order in which research was discovered.

For each section ask: **What must the reader understand here to accept the next section?** If a section would still make sense as a standalone LinkedIn post, its connection may be too weak.

### 3. Draft in continuous prose

Lead with the actual business situation and relevant tension. Use ordinary terms before introducing a specialized name. Develop each paragraph as an explanatory step: claim, relevant evidence, explanation, consequence, then a natural transition to the next idea.

Default to paragraphs. Use headings when the argument changes direction **or when the author explicitly organized the paper by named strategic priorities**. Use tables, bullets and exhibits **only if they convey relationships or comparisons more clearly than prose**. Do not turn a 12-page paper into 25 disconnected cards.

Prefer concrete nouns, active verbs, dates, named entities, clear units and explicit causal qualifiers. Avoid excessive scene-setting, false contrasts, repeated thesis statements and multi-adjective claims.

### 4. Separate understanding from recommendation

A strong paper distinguishes:
- **What is established:** sourced company behavior, observed outcomes and relevant external conditions.
- **What follows from those facts:** a reasonable interpretation, with qualifications.
- **What remains unknown:** the evidence required to discriminate between plausible explanations.
- **What should happen next:** a specific, bounded action justified by that analysis.

A hypothetical failure should be written as a possible risk, not as a diagnosis. It is acceptable for the correct conclusion to be **investigate, narrow, reuse an existing capability, or stop**.

### 5. Run four full-document edit passes

**Argument pass:** Remove material that doesn't advance the thesis; reorder paragraphs by dependence; eliminate repeated conclusions. Check whether each paragraph makes the next one necessary.

**Evidence pass:** Audit every number, date, title, attribution, causal claim, ownership statement, confidential detail and scope qualifier. Restore missing uncertainty; do not dilute established facts with needless hedges.

**Language pass:** Replace artificial business language with plain terms, remove first-person opinions, slogan-like phrases and unnecessary adjectives. Audit every paragraph for active voice, identifiable actors, direct verbs and unnecessary nominalizations. Preserve passive voice only where it improves factual accuracy or emphasis. Explain a technical term once, then use it consistently.

**Continuity pass:** Read the opening, first and last sentences of each paragraph, and ending as one sequence. If they do not form a coherent argument without headings, redraft. Then read the paper aloud or simulate a spoken executive explanation: it must sound natural, not performative.

See \`references/EDITORIAL_STANDARD.md\` for the editorial review and failure examples.

### 6. Issue the deliverable, not the scaffolding

Unless asked otherwise, return the actual complete paper, with an informative title, brief opening, proportionate sections, and an earned conclusion. Include a compact source note or properly placed citations as appropriate. Do not preface it with praise, methodological narration, a scorecard or options the user didn't request.

If important support is missing, state the uncertainty **within the paper** at the precise point where it matters. For high-stakes unverified claims, request source evidence rather than fabricating prose.

## Default paper architecture

Use these as conceptual functions, **not mandatory visible headings**. **If the author supplied a coherent structure, preserve it rather than imposing this default:**

\`Context → The decision problem → Evidence and mechanism → What current approaches explain / miss → Options and trade-offs → Recommendation or proof plan → Conclusion\`

A short board paper may compress several functions into a paragraph. A detailed white paper may expand them into substantial sections. Length does not justify extra frameworks, graphics or repetition.

## Writing standard

- Neutral third person by default, never "I think" or "we believe" unless explicitly requested.
- Favor active voice and identifiable actors; name who defines, authorizes, executes, reconciles, measures and changes each decision.
- Clear enough to read aloud. Prefer "what the systems can support" to "underlying operational infrastructure scalability capacity."
- Explain before naming. A new label is justified only if it makes a recurring distinction easier to understand.
- Favor one developed causal argument over stacked lists of observations.
- Avoid phrases such as "revolutionary," "transformational," "game-changing," "unprecedented," "unlock the future," "at the intersection of," "paradigm shift," and "compelling opportunity" without concrete evidence.
- Avoid formulaic "not X, but Y" constructions when both are actually true.
- No slogans, rhetorical self-congratulation or executive theater.
- Use uncertainty precisely: "may" and "could" for possible outcomes; "has" for documented actions; "would" for conditional effects.
- Avoid tables and bullets inside the main argument by default; retain them for genuine data comparison, options or decisions.
- No false references to official BCG, McKinsey or Harvard Business Review sponsorship. This is an independent writing method.

## Quality gate

Before delivery, check:

1. Can one sentence state the governing thesis without marketing language?
2. Does every section earn its place in the argument?
3. Are the evidence classes preserved and are assumptions explicitly testable?
4. Is every recommendation tied to a demonstrated problem or labeled hypothesis?
5. Does the prose stand on its own with the headings removed?
6. Would the paper sound like a competent executive explaining the issue plainly?
7. Is there any instance of "I think," "we believe," inflated claim language or needless jargon?
8. Are source citations, confidentiality and metric classes intact?
9. If the author supplied a structure or excerpt, does the revision preserve its strategic ambition, sequencing and distinctive operational examples?
10. Would any paragraph read like a detachable LinkedIn post, a generic consulting slogan, or a job pitch when the requested output is a research white paper?
11. Do sentences use active verbs and identify decision owners and operators where evidence supports them, without replacing unknown actors with invented ones?
12. If the paper includes both Purpose and Executive Summary, do they perform distinct functions instead of repeating the same definition?

If any critical check fails, revise before delivery. Do not report "passed" solely because the check is described. A sample exercise is in \`experiments/SHARKNINJA_STYLE_CHECK.md\` and is **not** proof of independent writing-quality gains.
