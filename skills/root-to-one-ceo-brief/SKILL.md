# Root-to-One CEO Brief

## Job

Turn an ambiguous strategic problem into a compact executive brief that makes the decision clearer before it makes the solution bigger.

The underlying Root-to-One sequence is:

`Unlock → Root → Reframe → Prove → Build to One → Graduate`

The public CEO-brief rendering is:

`Opportunity → Problem → Reframe → Mental Model → North Star → Visual → How We Learn → Real Example → Experiment → Decision → Build to One → Graduate → Restatement`

Do not write this like a LinkedIn post, pitch thread, or framework worksheet. Write it like a CEO strategy memo: compact prose, clear headings, few rhetorical flourishes, and explicit business choices.

## Core principle

A feature request is not a problem statement.

Do not begin with the proposed solution. First determine:

1. What are we actually trying to unlock?
2. What cannot be compromised?
3. What is the visible problem?
4. What is the root constraint?
5. Where in the system does that constraint actually sit?
6. Which constraints are fundamental, and which are inherited from the current architecture, workflow, organization, or business model?
7. What evidence would justify moving forward?
8. If the thesis survives, what must become real for this to count as a launched capability rather than an experiment?

## 1. Opportunity

State the business outcome first.

When possible, make the ambition concrete:

> **Illustrative target: improve / reduce / grow X by Y over Z while protecting [business invariant].**

A target can be illustrative rather than forecast. Label it clearly.

Do not force uniform improvement across a portfolio. A company-level target may require individual products, markets, channels, or businesses to move in different directions.

## 2. Problem

State the executive question as it exists today.

Good problem statements are recognizable, consequential, and solution-neutral.

Prefer:

> **Can we achieve X without sacrificing Y?**

over:

> **How should we build system Z?**

The visible problem is evidence. It may not be the root problem.

## 3. Reframe

This is the most important section.

Show why the original question is incomplete, at the wrong system layer, or constrained by an inherited assumption.

Useful pattern:

> **Instead of thinking about X as ______, think about it as ______.**

A valid reframe changes the decision or the system boundary. It is not merely a cleverer description.

Ask:

- Is the organization solving a symptom?
- Is the org chart being mistaken for the system?
- Is a current implementation being mistaken for a hard constraint?
- Is an A-or-B choice actually a false binary?
- Is the economic decision really allocation rather than elimination?
- Does the problem belong at a different system layer?

## 4. Mental Model

Give the executive a simple model that makes the reframe intuitive.

Good mental models include:

- portfolio;
- engine / fuel;
- system map;
- marketplace;
- flywheel;
- frontier;
- matrix;
- factory / manufacturer;
- control system; or
- before / after state.

The analogy must explain the mechanism. If it only makes the prose more colorful, remove it.

## 5. North Star

Translate the reframe into one strategic objective.

It should preserve the business invariant while making the desired economic or operating outcome explicit.

Examples:

- reduce total cost while protecting growth;
- increase monetization without degrading customer experience;
- reduce cycle time without weakening decision quality;
- expand the addressable market without transferring unacceptable risk.

Do not hide a compromise inside aspirational language.

## 6. Visual — required

Every completed CEO brief must contain at least one visual that makes the reframe understandable faster than prose.

Choose the smallest useful form:

- heatmap;
- decision matrix;
- before / after;
- system diagram;
- frontier curve;
- portfolio map;
- funnel;
- flywheel; or
- simple annotated table.

Default to a Markdown-native visual when the user has not requested a designed artifact.

The visual must explain the decision. Do not add decorative charts.

For portfolio problems, explicitly show that the aggregate target can move in one direction while individual cells move differently.

## 7. How We Learn

Describe the evidence loop before prescribing architecture.

Default:

`Measure → Experiment → Learn → Decide → Repeat`

Ask:

- What uncertainty matters most?
- What information would change the decision?
- What can be observed now?
- What must be tested causally?
- What can be learned without building a full product?
- What evidence would weaken or kill the thesis?

Do not confuse dashboards, attribution, usage, or correlation with causal proof when causality is the decision.

## 8. Real Example — required

Every completed CEO brief must contain at least one documented real-world example.

The example must do three things:

1. **What happened?**
2. **What does it teach us about this mechanism?**
3. **What does it not prove about the company or decision at hand?**

Prefer primary sources, published experiments, company disclosures, peer-reviewed research, or high-quality reporting.

If current web or research tools are available and the example is material, verify it before using it.

Never imply that an external example proves the target company has the same economics, customer behavior, causal mechanism, or result.

## 9. AI-native, when material

Do not make AI the thesis merely because AI is available.

Use AI when it materially improves one or more of:

- speed of learning;
- many-to-many reasoning;
- pattern detection;
- experiment prioritization;
- synthesis across evidence;
- decision quality;
- scenario generation; or
- operating leverage.

If AI is material to the differentiated capability, prefer an AI-native decision or learning loop rather than bolting AI onto a conventional workflow after the fact.

Do not prescribe a technology stack when the company may already possess the required infrastructure.

## 10. Experiment

Define the cheapest decisive test.

A strong experiment states:

- baseline;
- intervention;
- protected outcome;
- economic or operating outcome;
- duration or observation window when known;
- decision threshold; and
- failure / kill condition.

Prefer a narrow falsification test over a large MVP when the narrow test answers the load-bearing uncertainty more cheaply.

A prototype is not proof. Proof is not launch.

## 11. Decision

The output must enable a choice.

Examples:

`Accelerate | Maintain | Pulse | Wind down | Reallocate`

`Build | Buy | Partner | Wait | Kill`

`Proceed | Narrow | Reframe again | Stop`

Avoid ending with “more insights” or “build a dashboard.”

## 12. Build to One

Only after the thesis earns it, describe what must become real.

“One” means more than a prototype or promising test. It requires a real operating capability with enough architecture, integration, use, repeatable value, and credible economics to be considered launched.

Keep this section short in early-stage briefs. Do not invent architecture to make the memo look complete.

## 13. Graduate

Define the handoff condition.

Graduation is appropriate when the dominant uncertainty changes from:

> **Should this exist?**

to:

> **How aggressively should we scale it?**

At that point, ownership can move to the appropriate 1-to-100 operating team.

## 14. Say it again

End by restating the business transformation in one or two sentences.

Do not repeat the whole memo.

The closing should make the original problem feel simpler because the reader now sees the system differently.

## Writing standard

Use the internal discipline:

`Say it → Explain it → Detail it → Say it again`

Do not expose those labels in the finished memo unless the user explicitly asks for them.

Default style:

- CEO-level, not social-media copy;
- concise prose under clear headings;
- one governing idea per section;
- few bullets;
- no unnecessary slogans;
- no generic “AI transformation” language;
- no architecture dump before evidence;
- no fake precision;
- no unsupported company claims;
- no conversion of illustrative targets into forecasts.

## Value and behavioral checks

Before finalizing, ask:

- What does the decision-maker actually value?
- What are the strongest real alternatives?
- What should explicitly not be prioritized?
- Who controls the resource?
- If the strategy frees budget, capital, headcount, inventory, attention, or time, what actually happens to that resource?
- Does the strategy still work after realistic reallocation and second-order behavior?

Never assume a saved resource disappears.

For portfolio economics, distinguish:

`spend reduction | spend reallocation | budget expansion / contraction`

## Final checks

Before returning the brief, verify:

1. Is the business outcome clear before the solution?
2. Is the protected invariant explicit?
3. Did the reframe actually change the decision or system layer?
4. Is the mental model explanatory rather than decorative?
5. Is there at least one useful visual?
6. Is there at least one documented real example?
7. Does the example state what it does **not** prove?
8. Is the learning loop evidence-first rather than architecture-first?
9. Is the cheapest decisive experiment clear?
10. Does the memo end in a decision?
11. Did we avoid turning a prototype into “one”?
12. Is the graduation condition explicit?
13. Does the memo read like a CEO brief rather than a LinkedIn post?
